POV · Quick Feed CTA
The CTA is how we keep the promise.
A framework for where the user lands when they tap out of a clip.

Quick Feed is built around behaviour — mindless scrolling, low commitment, short bursts. Some clips are there to keep you on the feed. Some clips are there to build the watch commitment.

The CTA exists to bridge that idle browse into actual watch time. But the user could be in any of several mental states when they come into Quick Feed — a few flicks between a match, timepass, or looking to find their evening watch.

The clip is a promise. The CTA is how we keep it.

Section 01
The intent spectrum

Users arrive with a spectrum of intent. The same clip serves very different mental states. The challenge is picking a default that serves the widest set without betraying any of them.

"Show me what's happening here."

Curious, not committed
"Bookmark it. Maybe later."

Section 02
Two hypotheses

Two working hypotheses guide how we collapse the spectrum into a single default CTA.

Hypothesis 01
Do the harder task for the user
The asymmetry argument

If we take the user to the episode the clip is from, finding S1E1 is easy — it's right there in the player or details page. The reverse is not true. Landing on S1E1, then navigating back to the specific episode the clip came from, requires multiple steps.

The asymmetry is clear: the harder task is finding the clip's episode. We solve that. The easier task — navigating to S1E1 — the user can do themselves.

Default to clip's episode. S1E1 stays accessible, just not front-and-centre.
Hypothesis 02
Two CTAs, but use sparingly
The friction argument

Two CTAs means two decisions. On a Quick Feed where the goal is mindless sampling, every additional decision is friction at the wrong moment. A secondary CTA also produces misleading data — low taps look like "users don't want this option" when really they just didn't want to decide.

One CTA is the default. Where two are used, it should be for genuine forks where both intents are equally real — not a hedge because we're unsure.

Test two CTAs as an experiment, not as a blanket policy.

Section 04
Three experiments

We don't need to settle single vs. double CTA in one shot. Three testable variants — each with a clear hypothesis and what it tells us.

Experiment 01 · Recommended
Single CTA — clip's episode, always
Do the harder task for the user. One destination, one decision. Cleanest baseline.
Watch S3E8
Users wanting "start from beginning" find their own way. Mitigation: details page is one tap away.
Experiment 02
Two CTAs — clip primary, start from beginning secondary
Honour both intents. Primary is clip's episode; secondary gives the committed-viewer an explicit path.
Watch S3E8
Start from S1E1
Two decisions. Secondary may bleed clicks from primary without converting to longer sessions.
Experiment 03 · CW users only
Two CTAs for mid-watch — clip / resume
Restrict the two-CTA pattern to mid-watch users, where both intents are genuinely real.
Watch S3E8
Resume S2E5
Spoiler risk if clip is ahead of CW. Needs P13n guard upstream.
Sequencing
Start with Experiment 1 in Phase 1. Measure retention 30s post-tap, not just tap-through. High tap + fast bounce is the fingerprint of a misleading CTA. Layer Experiments 2 or 3 in Phase 2 based on what Phase 1 signals.
Section 05
Three things to always get right
01
Tell users where the clip is from
Show + S[x]E[y] or match name, always visible on the player. Independent of the CTA. Even if they don't tap, they know what they saw.
02
Make the label describe the destination
"Start from S1E1" / "Watch S3E8" / "Resume S2E5" / "Watch Live". The user reads it and knows what happens next. No "Watch Now".
03
Give two buttons only when there are actually two good answers
Most of the time, one button is right. Two buttons everywhere = noise. The second CTA earns its place only when both intents are genuinely real and equally weighted — not as a hedge because we're unsure.
